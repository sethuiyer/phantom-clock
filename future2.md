The key idea is stronger than “closed rays need a lap counter.”

A positive accumulated group delay cannot be represented as the difference of a globally defined function evaluated at the beginning and end of a closed ray.

## 1. The closed-orbit delay obstruction

Let \\(M\\) be a smooth optical state manifold, and let \\(\phi^t:M\to M\\) be a smooth ray flow generated by a vector field \\(X\\).

Let \\(f:M\to(0,\infty)\\) be a continuous delay accumulation rate, and define

\\[ D(x,T)=\int_0^T f(\phi^t x)\\,dt. \\]

Theorem. If the flow has a periodic orbit \\(\gamma\\) of period \\(P>0\\), there is no globally defined \\(C^1\\) function \\(u:M\to\mathbb R\\) satisfying

\\[ Xu=f. \\]

Proof. Suppose such a function exists. Along the periodic orbit,

\\[ \frac{d}{dt}u(\phi^t x) =(Xu)(\phi^t x) =f(\phi^t x). \\]

Integrating over one period,

\\[ u(\phi^P x)-u(x) =\int_0^P f(\phi^t x)\\,dt. \\]

But \\(\phi^P x=x\\), so the left side is zero. The right side is strictly positive.

Therefore

\\[ 0=\int_0^P f(\phi^t x)\\,dt>0, \\]

a contradiction. \\(\square\\)

The consequence is immediate: no instantaneous-state potential can reproduce positive accumulated delay along every trajectory when a closed ray exists.

This is not an implementation bug. It is a mathematical impossibility under the stated assumptions.

## 2. The deeper result: memory is a cocycle

Accumulated delay satisfies

\\[ D(x,T+S)=D(x,T)+D(\phi^T x,S). \\]

This is the additive cocycle identity.

If we could express the delay entirely through instantaneous states, it would have the form

\\[ D(x,T)=u(\phi^T x)-u(x). \\]

Such a cocycle is called a coboundary.

Our theorem proves that positive delay around a closed ray is not a coboundary.

That distinction gives us a clean mathematical definition of the problem:

\\[ \boxed{\text{Accumulated delay is a nontrivial dynamical cocycle.}} \\]

In other words, the optical state tells us where the ray is. The cocycle tells us what it has accumulated while getting there.

Those are fundamentally different kinds of information.

## 3. A concrete example

Take the simplest possible closed optical trajectory: a circle.

Let

\\[ M=S^1,\qquad \dot\theta=1. \\]

Choose a nonuniform delay rate,

\\[ f(\theta)=1+0.2\cos\theta+0.1\sin(2\theta). \\]

It is positive everywhere, so delay always increases.

After one revolution,

\\[ D(\theta,2\pi) =\int_0^{2\pi}f(\theta+t)\\,dt =2\pi. \\]

After three revolutions,

\\[ D(\theta,6\pi)=6\pi. \\]

Yet the optical state returns to exactly the same angle after each revolution.

I also numerically integrated this delay rate over a complete revolution. The result was \\(6.283185307179586\\), matching \\(2\pi\\) to numerical precision.

The experiment is elementary, but it verifies the mechanism without any complicated optical assumptions.

## 4. The stronger connection to Livšic theory

Here is where the mathematical depth increases.

For certain hyperbolic dynamical systems, Livšic-type theorems establish that vanishing integrals over every periodic orbit are sufficient, under appropriate regularity and dynamical hypotheses, for a function to be a coboundary.

Schematically,

\\[ \left[ \forall\gamma,\quad \oint\_\gamma f\\,dt=0 \right] \Longrightarrow f=Xu. \\]

Our positive group-delay observable fails the periodic-orbit condition immediately.

But the theorem also suggests a constructive decomposition.

Choose a reference accumulation rate \\(a\\), and ask whether

\\[ f-a=Xu. \\]

If this holds, then

\\[ D(x,T) = aT+u(\phi^T x)-u(x). \\]

The accumulated delay separates into two pieces:

\\[ \boxed{ D(x,T) = \underbrace{aT}\_{\text{elapsed-history contribution}} + \underbrace{u(\phi^T x)-u(x)}\_{\text{state-dependent correction}}. } \\]

For a periodic orbit of period \\(P\\), this requires

\\[ \oint\_\gamma f\\,dt=aP. \\]

Thus a single constant \\(a\\) works only if every periodic orbit has the same average delay rate.

More generally, the periodic-orbit averages constrain which history-dependent components can be eliminated through a change of representation.

This gives us a design principle for memory compression: remove the coboundary component, but retain enough history to reconstruct the non-coboundary component.

We must distinguish a mathematically minimal representation from a physically practical one. A general flow need not admit a single lap counter or a finite-dimensional history variable that works for all trajectories.
